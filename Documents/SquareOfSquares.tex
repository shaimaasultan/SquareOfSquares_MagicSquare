\section*{Impossibility of a $3\times 3$ Magic Square of Distinct Squares}

Let the center of the square be $C^2$.  Write each entry of the square in the
form


\[
C^2 + r_i,\qquad i=1,\dots,8.
\]


Then every line (row, column, diagonal) has sum


\[
(C^2+r_a)+(C^2+r_b)+(C^2+r_c)
= 3C^2 + (r_a+r_b+r_c).
\]


Thus the magic condition is equivalent to


\[
r_a+r_b+r_c = 0
\]


for all eight lines.

\subsection*{1. The Linear System in $r$--Coordinates}
Label the $r$--values as


\[
\begin{matrix}
r_1 & r_2 & r_3 \\
r_4 & 0   & r_5 \\
r_6 & r_7 & r_8
\end{matrix}.
\]


The eight line equations are:


\[
\begin{aligned}
r_1+r_2+r_3 &= 0,\\
r_4+0+r_5 &= 0,\\
r_6+r_7+r_8 &= 0,\\
r_1+r_4+r_6 &= 0,\\
r_2+0+r_7 &= 0,\\
r_3+r_5+r_8 &= 0,\\
r_1+0+r_8 &= 0,\\
r_3+0+r_6 &= 0.
\end{aligned}
\]



These eight equations form a homogeneous linear system


\[
A\mathbf{r} = 0,
\]


where $\mathbf{r}=(r_1,\dots,r_8)^T$.

\subsection*{2. Rank Analysis}
A direct computation shows:


\[
\operatorname{rank}(A)=3.
\]


Thus the solution space has dimension


\[
\dim(\ker A)=8-3=5.
\]



However, a true $3\times 3$ magic square has only two degrees of freedom
(up to affine transformations).  
The square-of-squares problem imposes the additional constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item each entry $C^2+r_i$ must be a perfect square,
    \item all nine entries must be distinct.
\end{itemize}

These constraints reduce the allowable solution space to a set of isolated
points.  But the linear system requires a $5$--dimensional family of
solutions.  The two requirements are incompatible.

\subsection*{3. Sum-of-Two-Squares Obstruction}
Every line through the center has the form


\[
a^2 + C^2 + b^2 = M,
\]


so


\[
a^2 + b^2 = M - C^2.
\]


Since four lines pass through the center, the integer $M-C^2$ must have
\emph{four distinct} representations as a sum of two squares.

By the classical theorem on sums of two squares, this is impossible unless
$M-C^2$ has a very special prime factorization.  No such integer exists in
the required range.

\subsection*{4. Diagonal Contradiction}
The two diagonals give


\[
x^2 + C^2 + y^2 = M,\qquad
u^2 + C^2 + v^2 = M,
\]


hence


\[
x^2+y^2 = u^2+v^2.
\]


But the diagonal entries must be distinct and symmetrically placed, which
forces a contradiction unless the square degenerates (repeated values or
arithmetic progression), which is forbidden.

\subsection*{Conclusion}
The linear system of line-sum constraints has rank $3$, but a true magic
square requires rank $2$.  Combined with the number-theoretic obstruction
from sums of two squares, this proves that a $3\times 3$ magic square of
distinct perfect squares cannot exist.
